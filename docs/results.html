WaveSpike-PPG · biometric identification and arrhythmia detection from pulse

WaveSpike PPG Results

How our spiking network (SNN) with a wavelet front end compares with paper-style methods we built ourselves (none is a paper's released code; see "Whose model is each one?"). Part 1 is person identification on two open PPG datasets, the current focus. Every number there is the average over 3 data splits × 2 random seeds, tested on sessions the model never saw. Part 2 is arrhythmia (AF) detection, the original goal.

The short version

  • Win
    On the harder across-days test (Zenodo wrist), our SNN with a fully learnable wavelet beats every classical method we built, our same-size GRU and our SNN without a wavelet, in 6 of 6 repeats.
  • Loss
    Our own CWT-CNN (a wavelet-image CNN we designed in the style of published CWT-CNN papers, not any paper's exact network) is more accurate on both datasets: 14.0% vs 15.6% error across days. It is about 4× bigger and needs roughly 5× more compute (estimate).
  • Mixed
    On Seeing Red (phone camera, 14 people), our beat-feature SVM (built in the style of that dataset's paper) is still the most accurate single model (19.6%). Our best SNN gets 21.8%.
  • Mixed
    A wavelet front end helps a lot (about 4–6 points less error than no wavelet). Making the wavelet learnable does not add accuracy: when only the wavelet changes, fixed, partly learnable and fully learnable score within about 0.5 points.
  • Loss
    Arrhythmia (AF) detection, the original goal: a simple 3-feature heartbeat-interval baseline scored 0.92 AUROC, against 0.67 for our SNN. On wrist data, every method including the baseline was at chance (about 0.5). That is why the project switched to biometrics.

How to read the numbers

EER, lower is better

Equal error rate: the error when wrongly-accepted and wrongly-rejected people are balanced. 0% is perfect, 50% is guessing.

1 window vs 5 windows

One decision from 10 s of pulse, or from 5 windows (about 50 s). Thick bar = 5 windows, thin line = 1 window.

Across sessions

Train on some sessions, test on a later one. Much harder than testing on the same session, which most papers do.

Accuracy and F1: "who is this?"

EER answers "is this person who they claim to be?" (yes/no). Accuracy and F1 answer "which of the enrolled people is this?" Top-1 accuracy is the share of decisions that name the right person. Macro-F1 averages each person's precision-and-recall score, so people with few recordings count equally. Higher is better for both. Guessing would score 5.9% on Zenodo (17 people) and 7.1% on Seeing Red (14 people).

    Each model is best with its own recipe, and the CNN at its best (14.0%) remains more accurate than our SNN at its best (15.6%). On Seeing Red the CNN wins 6 of 6 repeats under either recipe. So this result shows the comparison was fair; it does not show the SNN is more accurate.

Zenodo wrist AF · 19 patients · AF and normal rhythm in the same person · 1 repeat

Why the AF work stopped. In MIMIC PERform every window inherits its recording's AF label, so a model can score well by recognising the patient instead of the rhythm. The wrist dataset removes that shortcut, because the same person has both AF and normal stretches. There, every method, including the 0.92 baseline, fell to chance. Either 50 Hz wrist PPG is too noisy for heartbeat-interval features, or the AF labels are misaligned with the PPG windows. The label alignment was never checked.

Operation counts are rough estimates of dense multiply-adds, not measurements. *On spiking hardware, the SNN only does work when a spike fires, so its real cost would be lower. The Raspberry Pi benchmark will replace these with measured energy and latency.

Hardware-free energy estimates: running. edge/energy_estimate.py counts, per 10 s window, each model's parameters, dense multiply-adds, spike-driven additions (SynOps, measured spike rates) and neuron updates. It then applies four published energy models: 45 nm fp32 and int8 operation costs (Horowitz, ISSCC 2014, Fig. 1.1.9: 4.6 pJ per fp32 multiply-add, 0.9 pJ per add); the same plus SRAM weight reads (5 pJ per 32-bit weight); and Loihi neuromorphic costs (Davies et al., IEEE Micro 2018: 23.6 pJ per synaptic spike operation, 81/52 pJ per active/inactive neuron update). The SNN is scored both dense (as a CPU runs it today) and event-driven (only spikes do work). Results are saved to results/energy_estimates.json, so they can be compared later with energy measured on the Pi.

Were the models fully trained?

Not all of them. "Fully trained" here means the model ran its whole planned schedule: a fixed number of epochs with the learning rate decayed to zero. "Stopped early" means early stopping ended training once validation loss had not improved for 8 epochs (25 for the long variants). Training-loss curves were not saved for the biometric runs, so convergence itself is not verified, only that the schedule finished.

Why runs stopped early: the notebook was built for AF detection with early stopping on validation loss, to avoid overfitting a 35-patient dataset. On biometrics, validation loss bottomed out within a few epochs while EER kept improving, so early stopping cut training short. That is why every main biometric result uses the fixed 150-epoch schedule. A low best-on-validation epoch (3–50 on Seeing Red) means validation loss stops improving long before training ends; the last epoch was still the better model by EER.
